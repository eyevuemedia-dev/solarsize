How many solar panels do I need?

Short answer: divide the kWh you use in a year by what 1 kW of panels makes in a year where you live. The average U.S. home bought 863 kWh a month in 2024 (EIA), about 10,359 kWh a year. At the median state output of 1,367 kWh per kW (PVWatts), that is a 7.6 kW system, or about 19 panels of 400 W. By state, covering the local average home takes anywhere from 10 panels (California) to 27 (Washington).

This guide sizes a grid-tied home system for a target share of your yearly bill. It uses two published data sets: EIA's 2024 average residential use and bill for every state (EIA Electric Sales, Revenue, and Average Price, Table 5A), and the annual output of 1 kW of panels by state from NREL/NLR PVWatts v8 (state table). Off-grid cabins and RVs size differently (for the worst month, with days of battery), so see the off-grid cabin and RV solar guides for those.

The four-step method

  1. Add up 12 months of kWh. Use the "kWh used" line on each bill, not the dollar total, and add all 12 months so summer AC and winter heat both count. One month × 12 is a rough fallback. If you already have solar, your bill shows net purchases, not use; EIA flags the same problem in its own averages (EIA FAQ).
  2. Find your kWh per kW. Run your address in PVWatts with a 1 kW system, or use your state's figure in the table below. PVWatts already includes its default 14% system losses (soiling 2%, shading 3%, mismatch 2%, wiring 2%, connections 0.5%, light-induced degradation 1.5%, nameplate 1%, availability 3%) plus a 96% inverter (PVWatts system losses).
  3. System kW = yearly kWh × offset ÷ kWh per kW. Offset is the share you want to cover: 100% for a full net-metering offset, less under export-credit plans valued below retail (see below).
  4. Panels = system kW × 1,000 ÷ panel watts, rounded up. Then check roof space: about 21 sq ft per panel.

Worked example, Michigan: the average Michigan customer used 618 kWh a month in 2024 → 7,419 kWh a year. Michigan's PVWatts output is 1,291 kWh per kW. 7,419 ÷ 1,291 = 5.75 kW → 5,746 W ÷ 400 W = 15 panels, about 315 sq ft of panel area.

Worked example, Texas: 1,096 kWh a month → 13,154 kWh a year ÷ 1,421 = 9.26 kW, or 24 panels. Texas has more sun than Michigan, but the average Texas home uses 1.8× as much electricity, so it needs the bigger system.

What changes the panel count

  • Panel wattage. Residential panels now commonly run 400–440 W; the Qcells Q.TRON BLK M-G2+ series spans 420–440 W in the same 1,722 × 1,134 mm frame (Qcells). Higher-watt panels shrink the count, not the kW you need.
  • Roof direction and shade. PVWatts' default assumes only 3% shading loss from distant obstructions; trees or a chimney on the array need a site survey (PVWatts manual). An east- or west-facing roof makes less than a south-facing one; PVWatts lets you set azimuth and tilt.
  • Aging. After initial light-induced degradation, panels typically lose about 0.5% a year (PVWatts manual). That compounds to about 12% less output by year 25, so a system sized to exactly 100% today covers about 88% then. Adding a panel or two of margin is reasonable.
  • New loads. Plan for what's coming. Example (our assumption, not a rating): an EV driven 12,000 miles a year at 0.30 kWh per mile adds 3,600 kWh a year, about 7 more 400 W panels in Michigan or 7 in Texas. Check your car's rated kWh/100 mi on fueleconomy.gov. Heat pumps and induction ranges add load too.
  • Clipping and inverter size. Arrays are usually built with more DC panel watts than inverter AC watts; PVWatts' default DC-to-AC ratio is 1.2, and the annual kWh per kW already reflects that.

Should you size for 100%?

That depends on how your utility pays for exported power. With full retail net metering, a system that makes 100% of your yearly kWh roughly zeroes the energy part of the bill (fixed customer charges remain). Under export-credit tariffs, the math changes: California's Net Billing Tariff, for interconnection applications on or after April 15, 2023, credits exports at avoided-cost values that are generally low at midday (CPUC). Under rules like that, a smaller array matched to daytime use, or solar plus a battery that shifts midday output to evening, usually pays back better than a big array exporting cheap power. Ask your utility which tariff applies before choosing an offset.

How many batteries?

Batteries are sized by the kWh you want to use when the sun is down or the grid is out, not by panel count. The average U.S. home uses about 28.4 kWh a day. A Tesla Powerwall 3 stores 13.5 kWh and delivers up to 11.5 kW continuous with 185 A locked-rotor start capability (Tesla datasheet), so about two cover an average day for a whole home, while one unit can cover a smaller essentials-only load. For portable or DIY backup, size from the essentials you'd actually run:

  • How much battery storage do I need? (kWh, kW and motor-start sizing for home batteries)
  • Battery for 1 day of essentials and 3 days of essentials
  • Grid-tie vs battery backup: a grid-tie system without a battery shuts off in an outage.
  • LiFePO4 vs NMC vs lead-acid

Incentives in 2026

The 30% federal Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D) is not available for expenditures made after December 31, 2025, and for solar, the expenditure counts when installation is completed, so a system finished in 2026 does not qualify (IRS). State, local and utility rebates still exist in some areas; search your ZIP in DSIRE. The DOE's homeowner's guide to solar covers financing, permits and choosing an installer.

Measure before you buy

Your bill gives total kWh. A plug-in meter or a whole-home monitor shows which loads drive it, which helps when you cut usage first or plan battery circuits.

Questions

How many solar panels does the average house need?

The average U.S. residential customer bought 863 kWh a month in 2024 (10,359 kWh a year, EIA). At the median state output of 1,367 kWh per kW per year (PVWatts), that is about 7.6 kW, or 19 panels of 400 W. By state, covering the local average home takes from 10 panels (California) to 27 (Washington).

What is the formula for sizing a solar system?

System kW = annual kWh you want to cover ÷ annual kWh one kW of panels makes at your address (PVWatts). Panels = system kW × 1,000 ÷ panel watts. Use 12 months of bills for the kWh.

How many solar panels to produce 1,000 kWh a month?

12,000 kWh a year needs 6.8 kW in Arizona (18 × 400 W panels), 8.4 kW in Texas (22), and 9.3 kW in Michigan (24).

How much roof space do I need?

A 425 W-class residential panel such as the Qcells Q.TRON BLK M-G2+ measures 1,722 × 1,134 mm, about 21 sq ft. Multiply by the panel count, then allow extra for fire-code setbacks, vents and roof shape. The average Michigan home (15 panels) needs about 315 sq ft of panel area.

Should I size for 100% of my bill?

Only if your utility credits exported power at or near the retail rate. Under export-credit plans valued below retail, such as California's Net Billing Tariff for applications after April 15, 2023, a smaller system matched to daytime use, or solar plus a battery, often makes more sense. Check your utility tariff.

How many batteries do I need with solar?

Batteries are sized by kWh you want at night or in an outage, not by panel count. The average home uses about 28.4 kWh a day; one Tesla Powerwall 3 stores 13.5 kWh, so whole-home overnight coverage takes about two, while an essentials-only backup can be far smaller.

Is there still a 30% federal tax credit for home solar?

No, not for new installs. The IRS says the Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D) is not available for expenditures made after December 31, 2025, and the expenditure counts when installation is completed. Look for state, local and utility incentives in DSIRE instead.

Why is my installer quoting a different size?

Installers model your exact roof pitch, direction, shading and local weather, and may design for less than 100% offset to fit the roof or your utility's export rules. Our numbers assume an unshaded, typical fixed roof array; run your own address in PVWatts to compare.
