RV solar: how many watts and how much battery for boondocking?

Short answer: size RV solar for where and when you camp, with the panels lying flat on the roof. A light weekender (1,000 Wh a day, absorption fridge on propane) needs about 500 W of flat panels for a December at Quartzsite and about 200 Ah of LiFePO4. A full-timer with a 12 V compressor fridge, Starlink and a heated-humidifier CPAP (2,700 Wh a day) needs about 1,250 W flat, or 700 W tilted, plus 400–500 Ah. That's more than most roofs hold, so full-timers lean on a DC-DC alternator charger, a portable tilted panel and a generator. No realistic RV solar system runs a roof air conditioner.

RV solar is different from a house or a cabin in three ways: the panels usually lie flat on the roof, roof space runs out fast, and you move with the seasons. This guide uses the European Commission's PVGIS tool with the NREL National Solar Radiation Database to show flat-panel output at nine popular RV destinations in the season people actually go there, and builds the loads from maker spec sheets and owner measurements. For a fixed off-grid building, see the off-grid cabin guide instead.

Step 1: Add up daily watt-hours at 12 V

Count energy per day (Wh), and count it at the battery. Most RV loads are 12 V DC already, and anything on the inverter costs extra for conversion losses and idle draw. Divide watt-hours by 12.8 to get amp-hours at a 12 V lithium bank. These two example rigs use published figures; swap in your own.

  • The fridge choice is the big swing. An absorption fridge on propane only uses 12 V for its controls and gas valve (the Dometic RM2652 manual specifies a 3 A control fuse) (Dometic). Owners measure about 0.5–1.4 A (iRV2 thread). Switch it to 120 V and its heating element draws hundreds of watts, which no RV solar array keeps up with. A 12 V compressor fridge such as the Dometic DMC4101 needs 10.5–17 V DC and a 15 A supply (manual); one owner's Victron shunt logs showed 35 Ah a day in cool weather, about 50 Ah in the 70s°F and about 60 Ah in the high 80s (iRV2), and Mike Sokol's test reported by Let's RV ran a 100 Ah battery 20–25.5 hours depending on mode (Let's RV). That's 450–750 Wh a day.
  • The furnace blower counts in cold weather. Suburban's service manual lists 6.5 A for the SF-20/25/30 blower and up to 11.5 A for the SF-42 (Suburban). At 6.5 A, every hour the burner runs costs about 82 Wh, so a cold night with 8 hours of burner time adds about 655 Wh, about two-thirds more than all of Example A.
  • Inverter idle is a hidden load. Victron lists 12 W zero-load power for the Phoenix 12/1600 (10 W for the 12/1200) and 1.8 W in ECO search mode (datasheet). Left on 24 hours, that's 288 Wh, as much as the roof fan. Turn it off or use ECO mode when nothing AC is running.

These are long-term monthly averages; a week of clouds can come in well under them. PVGIS models free-standing panels; panels mounted flat on a hot roof run warmer (the Renogy 200 W panel loses 0.37% of output per °C, Renogy), which is part of why we add a 10% margin. Trees and canyon walls shade campsites far more than any house roof, so treat these as best-case open-sky numbers.

What the table says:

  • Snowbirds need tilt. At Quartzsite in Dec, a flat array makes 2.41 kWh per kW a day; tilted to 34° it makes 4.37, 81% more. The Florida Keys gain less (2.74 vs 3.78) because the sun is higher at 25° N.
  • Summer in the north is fine flat. At West Yellowstone, the worst summer month (Sep) is 3.68 flat vs 4.61 tilted. A 350 W flat array covers Example A.
  • The cloudy Pacific Northwest is the hard case. Forks, on the Olympic Peninsula, drops to 2.70 kWh/kW/day flat in Sep. Expect to use the generator or drive more.
  • Summer favors flat panels. At Quartzsite in June, flat beats tilted (6.17 vs 5.29), because the sun is nearly overhead. Tilt kits are a winter tool.

Step 3: Check your roof space

A Renogy 200 W 12 V panel is 58.7 × 27.5 × 1.38 inches and weighs 26.46 lb (Renogy), about 11.2 square feet per 200 W. So:

  • 400 W (2 panels) is about 22 sq ft, which fits on almost any trailer or van.
  • 800 W (4 panels) is about 45 sq ft, typical of a 25–30 ft trailer once you work around the roof AC, vents and antenna.
  • 1,200 W (6 panels) is about 67 sq ft and usually needs a large fifth wheel or motorhome.

Example B's 1,250 W for a Quartzsite winter would take 7 of those panels flat, which few roofs hold. Tilting cuts it to 4 panels. The practical answer is a mix: 600–800 W on the roof, a 200–400 W portable panel you can aim and park in the sun, and alternator charging on travel days. Each rigid panel adds about 26 lb plus mounts, so check your cargo capacity.

Step 4: Size the lithium bank

The battery carries you through the night and a cloudy day or two. For a 12 V LiFePO4 bank:

Battery Ah = daily Wh × days ÷ 0.90 ÷ 12.8 V
The 0.90 keeps a 10% reserve (our choice; Battle Born rates its cells for 100% usable depth of discharge, datasheet).
Example A, 1.5 days: 1,000 × 1.5 ÷ 0.90 ÷ 12.8 = 130 Ah, or 2 × 100 Ah.
Example B, 2 days: 2,700 × 2 ÷ 0.90 ÷ 12.8 = 469 Ah, or 5 × 100 Ah.

Check the battery's current limits as well as its capacity. A Battle Born BB10012 (100 Ah, 31 lb) is rated 100 A continuous discharge, 200 A for 30 seconds, and 50 A maximum charge (Battle Born). A 1,450 W inverter load draws about 134 A from a 12 V bank after losses, which is more than one battery's 100 A rating, so two batteries in parallel is the minimum for that inverter. On the charging side, 800 W of panels pushes about 55 A into a 14.4 V bank at full sun, which is fine for two or more batteries.

  • Cold charging. Battle Born's BMS blocks charging below 32°F (0°C) and allows discharge down to −4°F (manual). The heated BB10012H uses a 28 W internal heater to warm itself before charging. Put the bank inside the heated living space if you can.
  • Lead-acid. Flooded and AGM batteries are usually run to only about 50% to protect their life, so they need about twice the nameplate Ah and weigh far more. See LiFePO4 vs NMC vs lead-acid.

Step 5: Inverter, alternator and generator

  • Inverter. Size it for what runs at the same moment, not daily energy. The Victron Phoenix 12/1600 (120 V) is rated 1,450 W continuous at 25°C (datasheet), enough for a laptop, TV and a small coffee maker, but not a typical 1,500 W microwave plus anything else. Keep 12 V loads on 12 V: Starlink Mini accepts 12–48 V DC (Starlink), and ResMed sells a DC converter for the AirSense.
  • Alternator charging. A DC-DC charger lets the tow vehicle or chassis alternator charge the house bank safely. The Victron Orion-Tr Smart 12/12-30 is rated 30 A, about 430 W at 25°C and 360 W at 40°C (datasheet). A 4-hour travel day adds about 1.4–1.7 kWh, more than a 400 W flat array makes on most winter days.
  • Converter on shore power or a generator. A Progressive Dynamics PD4655 lithium converter puts out 55 A and draws up to about 950 W (Progressive Dynamics), so a 2,000 W inverter generator can refill an empty 400 Ah bank in about 7–8 hours of running. Run generators well away from windows, doors and vents, and keep the RV's CO alarm working.

What RV solar can't run

  • The roof air conditioner. Micro-Air's sizing examples put a 13,500 BTU roof AC at about 15 A on a hot day (Micro-Air), about 1,800 W. Eight hours at a 50% duty cycle is about 7.2 kWh, more than four 100 Ah batteries. Refilling that would take about 2,200 W of flat panels even in a Yellowstone Sep. Use shore power or a generator; our sister site has the roof AC, soft-start and generator math.
  • Electric heat and the fridge's 120 V mode. Space heaters, electric water-heater elements and the absorption fridge's AC element are resistive loads that use hundreds to over a thousand watts for hours. Use propane for heat and cooling when boondocking.
  • Induction cooking every day. A 1,800 W induction burner for 30 minutes is about 900 Wh, almost all of Example A's daily budget.

Recommended setups

  • Weekend summer camping (Example A loads): 400 W flat on the roof, 200 Ah LiFePO4, a 30 A solar charge controller and no inverter. That covers most northern summers. Add a 200 W portable panel if you camp in the trees.
  • Snowbird in the desert Southwest: 400–600 W on the roof with tilt kits, or flat panels plus a 200–400 W portable panel aimed south. Size the bank at 200–300 Ah. Tilt beats extra roof panels in winter.
  • Full-timer or remote worker (Example B loads): 800 W or more on the roof, 400–500 Ah LiFePO4, a Victron-class 1,500 W inverter, a 30 A DC-DC charger, and an inverter generator for cloudy stretches. Turn the inverter off or to ECO mode when not needed.
  • Want to skip the install? A portable power station plus folding panels covers a CPAP, lights and phones for a weekend. Our sister site compares power stations for camping and CPAP runtimes.

Medical devices: if you rely on a CPAP or another therapy device, keep a backup power plan and follow your clinician's and the device maker's instructions. Figures here are typical draws, not guarantees.

RV electrical work involves high currents. Fuse every positive cable at the battery, follow the battery and inverter makers' installation manuals, and have a qualified RV technician check the system if you're not sure.

Questions

How many watts of solar do I need on an RV?

Divide your daily watt-hours by your destination's worst-month output per kW of flat roof panels. Our 1,000 Wh/day weekender (absorption fridge on propane, fan, pump, CPAP) needs about 500 W of flat panels for a December at Quartzsite, Arizona. A 2,700 Wh/day full-timer with a 12 V compressor fridge, Starlink and a heated-humidifier CPAP needs about 1,250 W there, or 700 W with the panels tilted.

Is it worth tilting RV solar panels?

In winter, yes. PVGIS puts a flat 1 kW array at Quartzsite at 2.41 kWh a day in Dec, versus 4.37 kWh tilted to 34° facing south, 81% more. In June the flat array wins (6.17 vs 5.29) because the sun is nearly overhead. Snowbirds gain the most from tilt kits or a portable ground panel; summer campers can leave panels flat.

How much roof space do RV solar panels need?

A Renogy 200 W 12 V panel measures 58.7 × 27.5 inches, about 11.2 square feet, so 1,000 W of rigid panels covers about 56 square feet before you leave room for vents, the AC shroud and a walkway. Most travel trailers and vans fit 400–800 W; larger fifth wheels and motorhomes can fit more.

Can RV solar run the air conditioner?

Not in a useful way. Micro-Air's generator examples put a 13,500 BTU roof AC at about 15 A, roughly 1,800 W, on a hot day. Eight hours at a 50% duty cycle is about 7.2 kWh, which would need roughly 2,200 W of flat panels in a Yellowstone Sep on top of everything else. Run the AC from shore power or a generator.

How big a lithium battery does an RV need?

Size for one night plus a cloudy day or two. Our weekender (1,000 Wh/day) with 1.5 days of reserve needs about 130 Ah of 12 V LiFePO4 (2 × 100 Ah). The full-timer (2,700 Wh/day) with 2 days needs about 469 Ah (5 × 100 Ah). Battle Born rates its 100 Ah battery for 100 A continuous, so a 1,500 W-class inverter also needs at least two in parallel.

Can I charge RV lithium batteries below freezing?

Not standard ones. Battle Born's BMS blocks charging below 32°F (0°C), though the battery can discharge down to −4°F. Heated versions such as the BB10012H use a built-in heater (rated 28 W) to warm the cells before charging. Mount the bank inside the heated space if you camp in the cold.

How much does alternator charging add?

A lot, if you drive. A Victron Orion-Tr Smart 12/12-30 DC-DC charger is rated 30 A, about 430 W at 25°C and 360 W at 40°C, so a 4-hour drive adds roughly 1.4–1.7 kWh. That is more than 400 W of flat panels make on most winter days.

Does a CPAP work on RV solar?

Usually, if you budget for it. ResMed lists typical AirSense 11 use at about 7 W without the humidifier and about 55 W with the humidifier and heated tube, so 8 hours is about 56–440 Wh. Running it from 12 V through ResMed's DC converter avoids inverter losses. Always have a backup plan for a therapy device and follow your clinician's advice.
