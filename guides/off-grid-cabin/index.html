Off-grid cabin solar: how many panels and how much battery?

Short answer: size an off-grid cabin for its worst month, not the yearly average. Using real solar data for 10 cabin regions, a light weekend cabin (1,568 Wh a day) needs about 1.1 kW of panels (3 × 400 W) to get through December in Michigan's Upper Peninsula, and a full-time cabin with a full-size fridge, freezer and all-day Starlink (4,057 Wh) needs about 2.9 kW plus roughly 16 kWh of LiFePO4 for three cloudy days. Near Flagstaff the same full-time cabin needs 1.32 kW (4 × 400 W): 4,057 ÷ 0.85 ÷ 3.61 ÷ 1,000 = 1.322 kW, rounded to the table's 1.32. July, at 3.61 kWh per kW per day, is the worst steep-tilt month there. Keep a generator for long overcast spells, and keep lithium batteries above freezing.

Step 1: Add up your daily watt-hours

Off-grid sizing starts with energy per day (Wh), not watts. Multiply each load's watts by its hours of use, and use annual-average figures for anything with a compressor. Here are two example cabins built from published numbers. Swap in your own appliances; our appliance pages show where each figure comes from.

Two things stand out. First, refrigeration and always-on electronics dominate: the fridge, freezer, Starlink and inverter idle are about 66% of Example B. The 1,500 W microwave matters for inverter size, not daily energy. Second, the inverter's own draw is a real load: Victron lists 11 W at zero load for the MultiPlus-II 48/3000 (spec), which is 264 Wh a day before anything is plugged in. Search mode cuts that to 2 W when nothing is running.

Leave out what doesn't belong on a solar cabin system. Electric space heat, electric water heaters, electric ranges and central air are each several kilowatts, and running them from batteries in winter would multiply the array and bank several times over. Use propane or wood for heat, hot water and cooking.

Step 2: Find your worst-month solar output

A panel's daily output swings hugely with the seasons at cabin latitudes. PVGIS reports kWh per day for each month for 1 kW of panels after system losses (we used its default 14%). In Marquette, Michigan, a south-facing 1 kW array tilted at 62° makes about 3.85 kWh a day in July and 1.67 kWh in December (PVGIS 5.2). A system sized on the annual average of 3.31 kWh/kW/day would run short every winter day.

Panels (kW) = daily Wh ÷ 0.85 ÷ worst-month kWh per kW per day ÷ 1,000
The 0.85 is our planning allowance for inverter and battery losses. Victron's best-case inverter efficiency is 95% (spec), efficiency drops at light loads, and energy that passes through the battery loses a little more.
Example B in Marquette: 4,057 ÷ 0.85 ÷ 1.67 ÷ 1,000 = 2.86 kW, or 8 panels at 400 W.

Two adjustments help a lot in winter. Tilt steeper: raising the tilt from latitude to latitude + 15° lifts Marquette's December output from 1.57 to 1.67 kWh/kW/day. Check the horizon: PVGIS includes terrain shading from elevation data, but not trees. A cabin in a clearing surrounded by tall pines can lose most of its low winter sun, so stand where the array will go at midday in December, or use a sun-path app, before you buy panels.

What the table says: the cloudy north is the hard case. Western Washington's Cascades foothills (1.48 kWh/kW/day in December), the U.P. and the Adirondacks all need roughly two to three times the December array of Flagstaff or Leadville. Southern mountain cabins, such as the Smokies at 2.99, sit in the middle. If the cabin is mostly a summer place, size for the 3-season column and add capacity later.

Bend is already in the table: December is 2.45 kWh per kW at a latitude tilt, and the year is 1,540 kWh per kW. The Willamette Valley is not. A Portland point at 45.52°N, 122.68°W, south-facing at 46°, averages 1.46 kWh per kW on a December day and 1,129 kWh per kW per year under the same kind of PVGIS-NSRDB run (1 kW crystalline, 14% loss, free-standing, horizon on, checked November 6, 2026). That December rate sits next to Packwood's latitude-tilt December of 1.40, not next to Bend. Hawaii, Nebraska, and New Jersey are outside this cabin set. Their monthly south-facing runs, with the grid-tie annual math kept separate from a December design, are on those pages and on the Oregon page. A west-side Oregon cabin needs that December rate, rather than the statewide 4.06 peak sun hours.

Step 3: Size the battery bank

The battery covers nights and cloudy days. Pick how many days of autonomy you want, then divide by the usable fraction and the inverter loss:

Battery kWh = daily Wh × days ÷ 0.80 ÷ 0.95 ÷ 1,000
EG4 rates its 48 V LiFePO4 modules for daily cycling at 80% depth of discharge and recommends a 20% state-of-charge floor (EG4 spec sheet). The 0.95 is Victron's best-case inverter efficiency.
Example A, 2 days: 1,568 × 2 ÷ 0.80 ÷ 0.95 = 4.1 kWh. Example B, 3 days: 4,057 × 3 ÷ 0.80 ÷ 0.95 = 16.0 kWh, or 4 × 5.12 kWh modules (20.5 kWh).

  • Weekend cabin: 2 days is usually plenty, because the array refills the bank while you're away. Example A needs about 4.1 kWh, so one 5.12 kWh 48 V module or four 12 V 100 Ah LiFePO4 batteries (about 1.28 kWh each) cover it.
  • Full-time cabin: 3 days is a common starting point. Beyond that, each extra day of battery costs more than running a generator for a few hours, so most owners stop at 3 and keep a generator.
  • Chemistry: LiFePO4 suits daily cycling: EG4 rates its LL-S module for more than 6,000 cycles at 80% depth of discharge (EG4). Lead-acid banks are commonly run to only about half their capacity to protect their life, so they need roughly 60% more nameplate kWh for the same usable energy. See LiFePO4 vs NMC vs lead-acid.

Step 4: Pick the inverter and system voltage

The inverter is sized for peak watts, not daily energy. Add up what can run at the same time, then check motor starts. In Example B, the 1,500 W microwave alone uses most of a 2,400 W inverter such as the MultiPlus-II 48/3000, leaving about 900 W for the fridge compressor, Starlink, lights and laptop at the same moment. Its 5,500 W peak rating gives headroom for motor starts (Victron), but check your fridge's and pump's nameplate amps before you buy. Note the derating: the same unit is rated 2,200 W at 104°F and 1,700 W at 150°F, so give it a cool, ventilated spot.

Choose the battery voltage to keep current down. 2,400 W from a 12 V bank is about 200 A; from 48 V it's about 50 A, with much smaller cables and fuses. Small DC-only cabins can stay at 12 V: the SHURflo 4008 pump draws 7.5 A at most and runs directly from 12 V (Pentair), and the Starlink Mini takes 12–48 V DC input (Starlink), which also avoids inverter idle losses. The charge controller has to match both the array (voltage and current) and the battery; follow its maker's string-sizing tool.

Off-grid doesn't mean code-free. Many counties still require an electrical permit for a permanent solar and battery system. Have a licensed electrician design or inspect it, and follow the battery maker's installation manual for fusing, spacing and ventilation.

Cold, snow and generators

  • Lithium batteries can't charge below freezing. EG4 lists a charging range of 32°F to 122°F (0°C to 50°C); the LL-S battery management system stops charging below 23°F (−5°C) and resumes once the cells are back above 32°F (spec sheet). They can discharge down to −4°F. In an unheated winter cabin, keep the bank in an insulated, heated enclosure or buy batteries with built-in heaters.
  • Snow stops production. PVGIS doesn't model snow cover. A steep ground mount sheds snow faster and is easy to brush off; a low-slope roof array may stay buried for days.
  • A generator is the cheapest insurance. An inverter-charger turns a generator into a fast battery charger. The MultiPlus-II 48/3000 charges at up to 35 A, about 2.0 kW at 57.6 V absorption (Victron), so refilling 80% of a 20.5 kWh bank takes about 8 hours. Run any generator outdoors and away from windows and doors, and keep a battery-powered CO alarm in the cabin.
  • Starlink's snow melt uses power. Starlink lists snow-melt capability on the Mini (up to 1 inch an hour) (spec sheet). Expect higher draw during storms than the 25–40 W average.

Bottom line by cabin type

  • Summer-only cabin in the north: about 0.6 kW of panels (two 400 W panels) and one 5 kWh LiFePO4 module for Example A loads. No generator needed for most summers.
  • Year-round weekend cabin in the north: about 1.1 kW, the same 5 kWh bank, and a heated spot for the battery or a plan to charge only when you're there.
  • Full-time cabin in the north: about 2.9–3.2 kW, 20 kWh of LiFePO4 and a generator. Cut refrigeration and always-on loads first; each 100 W of continuous load adds 2.4 kWh a day.
  • Sunny Southwest or Rockies: less than half the northern array for the same loads.

Questions

How many solar panels does an off-grid cabin need?

Divide your daily watt-hours by the worst month's solar output per kW, then allow for inverter and battery losses. Our 1,568 Wh/day weekend cabin needs about 1.1 kW (3 × 400 W panels) to run through December in Michigan's Upper Peninsula, and 0.51 kW (2 × 400 W) near Flagstaff (1,568 ÷ 0.85 ÷ 3.61 ÷ 1,000 = 0.511 kW, rounded to the table's 0.51). July, at 3.61 kWh per kW per day, is the worst steep-tilt month there. A 3-season cabin that only runs May through September needs much less: about 0.5 kW in the U.P.

Why size for December instead of the annual average?

Because the battery has to be refilled every day of the worst month, not on average. In Marquette, a 1 kW array produces about 3.85 kWh a day in July but only 1.67 kWh in December at a steep tilt, per PVGIS. An array sized on the annual average (3.31 kWh/kW/day) would come up about half short in winter.

How big a battery does an off-grid cabin need?

Multiply daily use by the days of cloudy weather you want to ride through, then divide by the usable fraction. EG4 rates its 48 V LiFePO4 batteries for daily use to 80% depth of discharge. Our full-time cabin (4,057 Wh/day) with three days of autonomy needs about 16.0 kWh of nameplate battery, or 4 EG4 LL-S 5.12 kWh modules.

Can lithium batteries charge in an unheated cabin in winter?

Not below freezing, unless they have built-in heating. EG4 lists a charging range of 32°F to 122°F (0°C to 50°C), and the LL-S battery management system blocks charging below 23°F (−5°C) until it warms back to 32°F. Keep the battery bank in a space that stays above freezing, use self-heating batteries, or plan to charge only when the cabin is occupied and heated.

Do I still need a generator with off-grid solar?

Most year-round cabins in cloudy northern climates should have one. Long overcast spells can outlast any reasonable battery, and snow can cover panels. A generator feeding an inverter-charger refills the bank quickly: the Victron MultiPlus-II 48/3000 charges at up to 35 A, about 2.0 kW at 57.6 V, so refilling 80% of a 20.5 kWh bank takes roughly 8 hours.

Does the inverter use power when nothing is on?

Yes, and off-grid it adds up. Victron lists 11 W zero-load power for the MultiPlus-II 48/3000, which is 264 Wh a day, more than a ceiling fan on high all day. AES mode cuts that to 7 W and search mode to 2 W. A weekend cabin can switch the inverter off when nobody is there.

Should an off-grid cabin use 12 V, 24 V or 48 V?

Small DC-only cabins with a few lights, a pump and a 12 V fridge often stay at 12 V. Once you add an inverter of 2,000 W or more, 48 V keeps currents and cable sizes manageable: 2,400 W at 48 V is about 50 A, versus about 200 A at 12 V. Victron makes the MultiPlus-II in 12, 24 and 48 V versions with the same 2,400 W continuous rating.

What tilt angle is best for winter off-grid solar?

Steeper than your latitude. In PVGIS for Marquette, tilting to 62° instead of 47° raises December output from 1.57 to 1.67 kWh/kW/day while giving up some summer and annual energy. A steep, ground-mounted array is also easier to brush off after snow.
