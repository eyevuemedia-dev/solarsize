Grid-tie vs battery backup: what does solar do in a power outage?

Short answer: a plain grid-tie solar system goes dark in an outage. The inverter must stop exporting within 2 seconds of losing the grid, so the panels do nothing for you until power returns. Keeping the lights on takes one of three things: a daytime-only backup (SMA Secure Power Supply's 2,000 W outlet or Enphase Sunlight Backup), a battery that islands your home (one 13.5 kWh unit covers an essentials list for about 3.3 days with no sun, and solar refills it on most days), or a generator. For most homes, one or two batteries on a critical-loads panel is the sweet spot; a generator is the backstop for northern winters.

The question usually comes up right after the first outage with new panels on the roof: the sun is out, the array is rated 7 kW, and the house is dark. That's by design. This guide explains the rule behind it, lays out every way around it from a $0 outlet to a whole-home battery, and uses 11 years of hourly solar data from the European Commission's PVGIS tool to show whether panels can keep a battery topped up in the months when big U.S. outages actually happen.

Why a grid-tie system shuts off

Every utility-interconnected inverter in the U.S. is built to IEEE 1547. The 2018 revision requires an inverter that detects an unintentional island (your house energizing a dead grid) to cease energizing and trip within 2 seconds; a utility can extend that to 5 seconds (NREL IEEE 1547 primer, EPRI). When the grid comes back, the default return-to-service delay is 5 minutes of stable voltage and frequency before the inverter reconnects.

  • It protects lineworkers. A downed line that a crew assumes is dead could be back-fed by thousands of rooftop inverters through distribution transformers, which step 240 V back up to thousands of volts.
  • It isn't a setting you can override. Anti-islanding is part of the inverter's certification and your interconnection agreement. Backup systems work by physically disconnecting the house from the grid first (a transfer switch, Tesla's Backup Switch or Gateway, Enphase's System Controller, FranklinWH's aGate) and then forming a local grid for the inverters to follow.
  • Grid-forming vs grid-following. Standard string inverters and older microinverters are grid-following: they need a voltage and frequency reference to sync to. Battery inverters and Enphase IQ8 micros can be grid-forming, creating that reference themselves once the house is isolated (Enphase IQ8).

Daytime-only backup: useful, but know its limits

  • SMA Secure Power Supply. Sunny Boy US inverters have a dedicated outlet that, once you switch it on manually in an outage, delivers up to 2,000 W while the sun shines with no battery (SMA). If the load goes over 2,000 W or 20 A it shuts off and retries after 20 seconds, and output follows the clouds. SMA's hurricane-readiness post walks through using it after a storm (SMA).
  • Enphase Sunlight Backup. Pair IQ8 microinverters with an IQ System Controller 2 and at least one IQ Load Controller and the array can run up to four 240 V or eight 120 V essential circuits in daylight, with no battery (Enphase tech brief). Enphase supports up to 64 A / 15.4 kW of AC through the controller, and says whole-home backup isn't supported. The older IQ System Controller 1 isn't approved for this with IQ8, so check which one you have.
  • The catch. Our table below shows a 7 kW array often makes less than 5 kWh on a bad winter day in the north, and on a heavy-overcast hour the output can't start a fridge compressor. Treat daytime-only systems as a bonus, not a plan, and add a battery later; both SMA and Enphase systems can.

Can solar recharge you during a real outage?

This is where the average-day numbers in most calculators mislead. Outages cluster in storms, and storms come with clouds. What the numbers say:

  • Gulf and Florida hurricanes: solar keeps essentials going. After Beryl, Houston's bad July day still gives a 7 kW array about 19 kWh, about four times the essentials need. Tampa in October: 19 kWh on a bad day. Post-hurricane outages run for days, but the skies usually clear, so a single battery plus solar can usually carry the fridge, freezer, sump pump and internet for as long as the outage lasts. Central AC is another matter: see the half-home column.
  • Mountain storms are cloudier. Asheville in September: median 30 kWh, bad day 9.1 kWh, worst day 0.0 kWh. Enough for essentials most days, not all.
  • Winter storms break solar-only plans. Dallas in February has a bad day of 6.5 kWh and a worst of 1.4 kWh, and that's before snow or ice on the panels, which PVGIS doesn't model. Detroit's bad January day is 2.6 kWh and Boston's 2.6 kWh, so a battery only stretches its stored energy. For northern winters, a battery plus a generator (or a generator alone) is the honest answer.
  • West: shutoffs come with sun. Sacramento's October PSPS season is clear: a bad day is still 20 kWh. Denver in December: 7.1 kWh on a bad day, median 22 kWh.
  • Half-home loads are a weather bet. On a median day, every metro except Detroit in January covers 14.2 kWh from a 7 kW array. On a bad day only Houston, Tampa and Sacramento do. Running AC or electric heat through a cloudy week takes more battery, more panels or a generator.

How long outages last matters too. EIA's 2024 reliability data puts the average U.S. customer at 662.6 minutes (about 11 hours) without power for the year including major events, and 131.6 minutes without them. Major events (Beryl, Helene and Milton in 2024) were about 80% of the total, and the average interruption lasted 432.7 minutes, about 7.2 hours, when major events are counted (EIA Table 11.1, EIA Today in Energy). So for most homes the typical outage is a single evening or night, which one battery handles easily; the long tail is the multi-day storm.

Battery specs that matter for backup

Capacity (kWh) decides how long; continuous power (kW) decides how much at once; and surge or locked-rotor amps (LRA) decide whether a motor can start. Central AC compressors and well pumps are where backup systems most often fail on day one.

AC-coupled vs DC-coupled (hybrid)

  • AC-coupled batteries have their own inverter and connect on the AC side, next to your existing solar. Enphase IQ Battery 5P (six IQ8D-BAT microinverters inside) and FranklinWH aPower 2 work this way. They're the usual retrofit for an existing grid-tie system, because the panels and inverter stay. The installer has to confirm the battery system can control your existing inverters' output when the house is off-grid; otherwise a full battery and a sunny noon have nowhere to send the surplus.
  • DC-coupled or hybrid systems put the panels and battery behind one inverter. Powerwall 3 accepts up to 20 kW of DC solar directly and rates 97.5% solar-to-home efficiency. For a new install, that's one inverter instead of two; for a retrofit, it usually means replacing or bypassing the old inverter.
  • Either way you need a gateway or transfer device that separates the house from the grid, and a licensed electrician. A critical-loads subpanel or a smart panel (EnergySage names Span, Lumin and Schneider) decides which circuits stay on when the battery can't cover the whole home (EnergySage).

Costs and incentives in 2026

  • Batteries. EnergySage's July 2026 data puts a typical 13.5 kWh installed battery at about $15,647 before incentives and whole-home backup at about $34,000 (EnergySage). By brand, per kWh: Tesla about $967, FranklinWH $1,232, EG4 $1,149 and Enphase $1,429. By state: Florida about $1,005/kWh, Texas $1,059, California $1,079 and Michigan $1,364.
  • Market. EnergySage's H1 2026 report shows a median battery price of $1,130/kWh, a median solar price of $2.57/W, and a 31% battery attachment rate on quotes (EnergySage H1 2026).
  • Federal credit. The 30% residential credit for purchased solar and batteries ended January 1, 2026 under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. Third-party-owned systems (leases and PPAs) can still get federal incentives through 2027, and EnergySage found pre-paid leases about 39% cheaper per kWh than cash (EnergySage). It also flags Section 232 polysilicon tariffs starting December 4, 2026, which may push panel prices up.
  • Payback. Backup has no payback of its own; it's insurance. A battery can earn money on time-of-use rates, which we model utility by utility in home battery payback on TOU rates.

Which should you choose?

  • Outages are rare and short (a few hours a year): stay grid-tie only, or use SMA SPS or Sunlight Backup if your inverters support it. Keep a portable power station for the fridge and internet.
  • Hurricane country (Gulf, Florida, Carolinas): one or two batteries on a critical-loads panel, plus solar. The data above says the sun comes back fast enough to keep essentials running for weeks. Add a generator inlet if you want AC.
  • Cold-climate winter storms (Midwest, Northeast, Plains): a battery for the short outages and a generator with an interlock or transfer switch for multi-day ice storms. Size the generator with our sister site's generator sizing guide.
  • Well pump, central AC, electric heat, or medical equipment: size for the biggest motor start first, then for energy. That usually means two or more batteries, or a battery plus generator.
  • Off-grid entirely? That's a different design; see off-grid cabin sizing or RV solar sizing.

Medical devices: if you rely on a CPAP, oxygen concentrator or other powered medical equipment, don't plan around daytime-only solar or average weather. Talk with your clinician or equipment supplier about a backup plan, and ask your utility about medical-needs registration. Our sister site lists CPAP runtimes on portable power stations. Figures here are planning estimates, not guarantees.

Any backup system that connects to your home's wiring must be designed and installed by a licensed electrician or installer and approved by your utility. Never back-feed a home through a dryer outlet with a "suicide cord"; it's illegal and can kill lineworkers.

Questions

Does grid-tied solar work during a power outage?

Not on its own. IEEE 1547-2018, the interconnection standard U.S. utilities use, requires a grid-tied inverter to detect an unintentional island and stop energizing the line within 2 seconds (utilities may allow up to 5). That protects lineworkers repairing the grid. To keep power you need a system that can disconnect from the grid first: a battery with a transfer switch or gateway, or a daytime-only backup such as SMA Secure Power Supply or Enphase Sunlight Backup.

Can I use solar panels without a battery during an outage?

Only in limited ways. SMA Sunny Boy US inverters with Secure Power Supply can feed up to 2,000 W to one dedicated outlet while the sun is up. Enphase Sunlight Backup uses IQ8 microinverters and an IQ System Controller 2 to run up to four 240 V or eight 120 V essential circuits in daylight. Both stop at sunset and sag under clouds, so neither covers a fridge overnight or a CPAP at night.

How long will one Powerwall 3 last in an outage?

Tesla rates Powerwall 3 at 13.5 kWh. Our essentials list (fridge, chest freezer, sump pump, Wi-Fi, a TV, a laptop, a ceiling fan and a CPAP) uses about 4.1 kWh a day, so one unit covers about 3.3 days with no sun. An illustrative half-home load of 14.2 kWh a day drains it in about 23 hours. With solar recharging, the answer depends on weather; see the table on this page.

Can solar recharge a battery during a long outage?

Usually, for essentials. In PVGIS hourly data, a 7 kW array near Houston makes about 19 kWh even on a bad July day, enough for the essentials list. In winter the north is different: near Detroit, a bad January day yields about 2.6 kWh and the worst day in 11 years about 0.7 kWh, so a battery-only plan can run out. Snow on the panels makes it worse.

Do I need a whole-home battery?

Most homes don't. EnergySage puts whole-home backup at about $34,000 versus $15,647 for a typical 13.5 kWh battery before incentives. A critical-loads panel or smart panel lets one or two batteries run what matters. Whole-home backup makes sense for central AC, well pumps and electric heat, or when outages are long and frequent.

Is there still a federal tax credit for home batteries?

Not for cash or loan purchases. EnergySage reports that the One Big Beautiful Bill Act ended the 30% residential clean energy credit on January 1, 2026. Leases and power purchase agreements (third-party ownership) can still carry federal incentives through 2027. Check your state and utility for rebates.

AC-coupled or DC-coupled battery: which is better?

If you already have solar, an AC-coupled battery (Enphase IQ Battery 5P, FranklinWH aPower 2) usually adds on without replacing your inverter. For a new install, a hybrid or DC-coupled system such as Tesla Powerwall 3, which has a built-in solar inverter rated 97.5% solar-to-home, uses one inverter for both. The installer must confirm the battery can control your existing inverters when off-grid.

What about a CPAP or oxygen concentrator?

Plan for night and for the worst day, not the average. Daytime-only systems don't run a CPAP at night. A battery or a portable power station can, but medical devices need a backup plan you have discussed with your clinician or equipment supplier, and many utilities offer medical-needs registration. Our sister site lists CPAP runtimes by power station.
