How many solar panels to run a microwave?

Fewer than you think, but the inverter matters more than the panels. Ten minutes a day on full power is about 250 Wh, which takes about 67 W of panels at the U.S. average. The catch is the 1,500 W draw while it runs: you need an inverter or power station rated above 1,500 W continuous and a battery bank that can deliver about 130 A at 12 V, or 33 A at 48 V.

Short answer

  • Power: a "1,100 W" microwave like the Panasonic NN-SU656 draws 1,500 W from the outlet. The 1,100 W is cooking output (Panasonic specs).
  • Energy: 125 Wh for 5 minutes, 250 Wh for 10, 375 Wh for 15. Panels: about 33 W, 67 W and 100 W at U.S. average sun; roughly 2.3× that in a northern December.
  • Inverter: pick one rated at least 1,800–2,000 W continuous, pure sine wave. A 1,500 W unit is right at its limit.
  • Battery: at 12 V the bank must supply about 130 A. A single 100 A-continuous lithium battery is not enough; use two in parallel, a higher-current battery, or a 24 V or 48 V system.
  • Idle trap: the microwave's clock is capped at 1.0 W by federal standby rules, but leaving a 15 W inverter on all day to power it costs about 360 Wh, nearly as much as 15 minutes of cooking. Switch the inverter off between uses.
  • Our pick: for a cabin, van or outage kit, a 1 kWh-class power station with 1,800 W continuous (DELTA 2, SOLIX C1000, AC180) and 200 W of panels. For a built-in system, 24 V or 48 V with a 2,000 W+ inverter.

Power vs energy: why a microwave is a sizing outlier

Most appliances on this site are energy problems: a fridge cycles all day and night, so you size panels and battery for the daily total. A microwave is the opposite. It pulls a lot of power for a few minutes, so the daily energy is small and the instantaneous load is large.

Panasonic lists the NN-SU656 at 120 V, 60 Hz, with 1,100 W of microwave output and 1,500 W of power consumption (Panasonic). That 400 W gap is heat in the magnetron and power supply; it is why the label on the back, not the number on the box, is what to size for. About 73% of the input becomes heat in the food. Look for "input" or "power consumption" on your own model's label.

Standby power and the inverter idle trap

Federal efficiency standards cap microwave standby power. Countertop microwave-only and countertop convection models made from June 17, 2016 must average no more than 1.0 W in standby, and models made on or after June 22, 2026 no more than 0.6 W; built-in and over-the-range convection models are capped at 2.2 W, then 1.0 W (10 CFR 430.32(j)(3)). So the clock itself costs at most about 24 Wh a day.

The inverter that feeds it is another story. Power stations and standalone inverters burn power just being on: BLUETTI lists about 15 W of AC self-consumption for its AC180, and Willrun's station data shows 15–40 W across popular models. Left on 24 hours just to keep a microwave clock lit, a 15 W idle is about 360 Wh, roughly what the microwave uses in 15 minutes of cooking. Turn the inverter on when you cook, or use a station with an AC auto-off timer.

Battery current: the part DIY systems get wrong

At 12 V, a 1,500 W load is a lot of current. This is the number that decides cable size, fuses and whether your battery's BMS lets it happen at all.

12 V (12.8 V LiFePO4)12.8 V130 ANo: over 100 A continuous

48 V (51.2 V)51.2 V33 AYes

DC amps = 1,500 W ÷ 0.9 inverter efficiency ÷ nominal voltage. Current rises as the battery voltage sags under load. Battle Born's BB10012 (12 V, 100 Ah) lists 100 A continuous and 200 A for 30 seconds (data sheet), so a single one would hit its limit in about half a minute.

  • 12 V systems: two 100 A-continuous batteries in parallel, or one rated 150–200 A continuous, plus cable and fusing sized for the inverter maker's chart. A 2,000 W 12 V inverter on undersized cable will sag and alarm even with a full battery.
  • 24 V and 48 V systems: the current halves or quarters, so standard batteries and cables handle a microwave easily. If you are building new and want a microwave, this is the reason to go 24 V or 48 V.
  • Lead-acid: high current drops usable capacity and voltage, and the inverter's low-voltage cutoff arrives early. Budget extra capacity if you are on AGM or flooded batteries.

Two things stand out. First, a "1,000 Wh" station is not enough on its own: what matters is the continuous AC rating, and anything under 1,500 W simply trips. Most of the 1,800 W stations here accept 200 W or more of solar, which refills a few microwave sessions in a sunny afternoon.

Making it work on a small system

  • Cook at full power in shorter bursts rather than long sessions at 50%: the energy is similar, but you finish while the sun is up and the battery is full.
  • Run it while the panels are producing. Midday solar covers part of the draw directly and shortens the battery's high-current stretch.
  • One big load at a time. Microwave plus kettle or coffee maker on one inverter is 2,500 W or more. Stagger them.
  • Measure your own unit with a plug-in watt meter for a week. Most people overestimate minutes; the meter gives you a real Wh-per-day to size against.
  • Smaller microwaves draw less, but check the input rating on the label rather than the output watts in the ad.

What we'd build

  • Van, RV or outage kit: a 1 kWh-class power station with 1,800 W continuous, such as the EcoFlow DELTA 2, Anker SOLIX C1000 or BLUETTI AC180, plus a 200 W portable panel. That covers 10–15 minutes of microwave use a day with the fridge and lights on top in summer sun. Willrun's RV power station guide covers the full load list.
  • Off-grid cabin: size the panels and battery for the fridge and lights first (our off-grid cabin guide), then make sure the inverter is 2,000 W+ continuous and the bank is 24 V or 48 V. The microwave adds only 150–250 W of panels.
  • Grid-tied home with a backup battery: a microwave is not a sizing concern for whole-home batteries; check that the backed-up circuit includes the kitchen. See how much battery storage you need.

Questions

How many solar panels to run a microwave?

For about 10 minutes a day on full power, a 1,500 W-input microwave uses about 250 Wh, which is about 67 W of panels at the U.S. average of 4.98 sun hours and about 154 W in a northern December. The panels are the easy part; the inverter must be rated above 1,500 W continuous.

Where does the daily energy come from?

Panasonic lists the NN-SU656 at 1,500 W power consumption and 1,100 W microwave output at 120 V. Daily energy is 1,500 W times the minutes you run it on full power. The original 375 Wh figure on this page assumes 15 minutes a day; a power-level setting below 100% cycles the magnetron and uses proportionally less.

What size inverter do I need for a microwave?

At least the microwave's input rating with headroom: for a 1,500 W input model, use a pure sine wave inverter or power station rated about 1,800 to 2,000 W continuous. A 1,500 W inverter is at its limit, and boost modes for heaters are not a substitute.

Can a 100Ah lithium battery run a microwave?

Not a single 12 V battery limited to 100 A continuous. A 1,500 W microwave pulls about 130 A at 12.8 V through a 90%-efficient inverter. Battle Born's BB10012 allows 200 A for only 30 seconds. Two in parallel, a higher-current battery, or a 24 V or 48 V bank will do it.

How long will a power station run a microwave?

About capacity × 0.85 ÷ 1,500 W of full-power time, if the station's continuous output is above 1,500 W. A 1,024 Wh EcoFlow DELTA 2 gives about 35 minutes; a 2,048 Wh DELTA 2 Max about 70 minutes.

Does a microwave use power when it's off?

Yes, a little. Federal rules cap standby at 1.0 W for countertop models made since mid-2016 and 0.6 W for those made from June 22, 2026. The bigger loss on solar is leaving an inverter on to power the clock, which can be 15 W or more.
