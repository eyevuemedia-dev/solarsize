How many solar panels to run a chest freezer?

A chest freezer is one of the easiest appliances to put on solar. ENERGY STAR's median 7 cu ft compact chest uses about 616 Wh a day, and a 9–10 cu ft full-size chest about 584 Wh. At the U.S. average of 4.98 sun hours that is roughly 165 W of panels; in a Detroit December it is about 379 W. A day of battery is about 0.76 kWh. The catch is the start: Haier lists 759 W of start-up draw for its 5 and 7 cu ft chest freezers, about four times the 196 W running draw, so the inverter has to handle the surge. Our recommendation for an off-grid 7 cu ft chest: 400 W of panels, a 1.5–2 kWh LiFePO4 battery and a pure sine inverter or power station with at least 1,000 W of rated surge.

Daily energy, panels and battery by freezer size

The ENERGY STAR kWh/yr figure is the best published number for a freezer's real energy use: it comes from the federal lab test and already includes the compressor cycling on and off. Divide by 365 for Wh per day. We took the median of every certified model in each size band, so one unusually efficient or thirsty model doesn't set the answer.

Two things stand out. First, the compact class has a looser federal limit, so a 7 cu ft compact chest (median 225 kWh/yr) uses more than a 9–10 cu ft full-size chest (median 213 kWh/yr). If you have the floor space, the bigger chest costs nothing extra on solar. Second, six times the volume (3.5 to 21.7 cu ft) only doubles the energy, so a bigger chest costs little extra on solar.

Startup surge: the part that trips small inverters

ENERGY STAR doesn't publish running or startup watts, but Haier publishes both for several of its chest freezers (Haier: refrigeration power consumption):

HF71CW20W7.1 cu ft chest6.6 A / 759 W1.7 A / 195.5 W

Haier support article, checked 2026-11-20 (watts = amps × 115 V as listed by Haier). Other brands will differ; check the rating plate or ask the maker.

  • Inverter continuous rating: at least 1.25 × running watts, so about 245 W for the Haier 7 cu ft. Almost any inverter clears that.
  • Surge: the inverter must deliver the ~760 W start for a fraction of a second, every time the compressor kicks on, many times a day. Pick one whose rated surge is at least 1,000 W, and prefer makers that say the surge applies to motor or compressor loads. Some small stations advertise a "boost" mode that only works for resistive loads like kettles.
  • Pure sine wave only. Compressors run hotter and noisier on modified-sine inverters.
  • Duty cycle check: 225 kWh/yr is an average of 25.7 W. Against 195.5 W running, that implies the compressor runs roughly 13% of the time in the lab test, or about 3 hours a day. Expect more in a hot garage and right after loading warm food.

Willrun's fridge outage guide applies the same idea to refrigerators and lists which power stations publish a usable motor surge figure.

A northern December needs two to three times the summer array. Snow on the panels takes output to zero. A freezer in an unheated garage or cabin does use less energy in winter, which helps, but don't count on it in your sizing; size for your freezer's measured use in the worst month, or plan a generator or grid recharge for the darkest weeks.

Outage backup vs off-grid: two different jobs

Outage backup. FoodSafety.gov says a full freezer holds a safe temperature for about 48 hours (24 hours if half full) with the door closed (FoodSafety.gov). So in a short outage a full chest freezer needs no power at all. In a long one, a power station that runs it for part of each day, recharged by panels, keeps it frozen with far less battery than the 3-day column suggests. Keep it closed and keep a thermometer inside; food with ice crystals or at 40°F or below can be refrozen.

Off-grid. The freezer runs every day, all year. Size for the worst month in the table above, add at least one day of battery for cloudy weather (two in the north), and check the inverter surge. For a 7 cu ft chest that lands at about 400 W of panels and 1.5 kWh of battery for two days, before any other loads.

Running a freezer alongside the rest of a cabin? Add it to the full load list in the off-grid cabin guide or the calculator; the battery and inverter are shared, so the freezer's surge has to fit on top of whatever else is running.

Chest vs upright on solar

The median 11–16 cu ft auto-defrost upright uses 396 kWh/yr, about 1.9 times a 9–10 cu ft chest. Two reasons: cold air spills out of an upright door every time you open it, and auto-defrost runs a heater several times a day. On solar that difference is about 134 W of extra panels at the U.S. average and about 0.6 kWh more battery per day of backup. Manual-defrost chest freezers are the solar choice; the trade-off is defrosting by hand once or twice a year.

Where these numbers can be wrong

  • Location. ENERGY STAR figures come from a fixed lab temperature. A freezer in a hot garage or an RV in summer uses more; one in a cool basement uses less.
  • Use. Frequent opening, warm food loads and a half-empty freezer all raise energy use.
  • Age. Older freezers, worn lid seals and ice buildup raise it too. A freezer from before current efficiency standards can use far more than these medians.
  • Measure it. A plug-in watt meter for a full week gives you your own Wh per day; use that number instead of ours in the calculator. If you do, send it in with a photo and we'll compare.

What to buy for a 7 cu ft chest freezer

  • Panels: 400 W total (for example two 200 W panels). Covers a northern December on paper and leaves summer headroom for other loads. In the Sun Belt, 200–300 W is enough year-round.
  • Battery: 1.5–2 kWh of LiFePO4 for two days without sun. As a portable power station, that is the 2 kWh class.
  • Inverter: pure sine, at least 300 W continuous and 1,000 W rated surge that applies to motor loads. Many 1 kWh-plus power stations publish 2,400 W or more; check that the figure applies to motors.
  • Freezer itself: if you're buying new for solar, a manual-defrost full-size chest around 9–10 cu ft uses no more energy than a 7 cu ft compact (see the table).

Questions

How many solar panels to run a chest freezer?

About 165 W for a typical 7 cu ft compact chest freezer at the U.S. average of 4.98 sun hours, based on ENERGY STAR's median 225 kWh a year (616 Wh a day). In a northern December, plan on about 400 W. A 9–10 cu ft full-size chest needs about the same.

How many watts does a chest freezer use?

Average draw is low: about 26 W for a 7 cu ft compact chest across the year. While the compressor runs it is higher; Haier lists 195.5 W running and 759 W at start-up for its 5 and 7 cu ft chest freezers.

What size battery do I need for a chest freezer?

About 0.76 kWh per day for a 7 cu ft chest after allowing for 90% depth of discharge and 90% inverter efficiency. For two days without sun, about 1.5 kWh; a 2 kWh LiFePO4 battery or power station is a sensible size.

What size inverter do I need for a chest freezer?

At least 1.25 times the running watts (about 245 W for a 7 cu ft Haier) and a rated surge of at least 1,000 W that applies to motor loads, since Haier lists about 760 W at start-up. Use a pure sine inverter.

Does a chest freezer use less energy than an upright?

Yes. ENERGY STAR's median 9–10 cu ft chest uses 213 kWh a year; the median 11–16 cu ft auto-defrost upright uses 396 kWh, nearly twice as much.

Can a chest freezer run on solar in winter?

Yes, if the array is sized for the worst month. A 7 cu ft chest needs about 380 W of panels in a Detroit December and about 560 W in Seattle, versus about 130 W in July. Snow on the panels stops output entirely, so northern off-grid systems need a backup charge source.

Where does the daily energy come from?

From the ENERGY STAR certified freezers list: we take the median annual kWh of all certified models in each size band and divide by 365. The original example on this page, the 9.0 cu ft Whirlpool WZC3209LW, is listed at 201 kWh a year, about 550 Wh a day.
