How 384 Wh, 103 W, and 0.47 kWh are calculated

The spec table is one television, not every 55-inch set. ENERGY STAR lists 76.9 W on-mode for LG model family 55QNED85TU*, at a 54.6-inch viewable diagonal, and 0.30 W standby both with the network disconnected and with it connected (certified televisions dataset). Five hours a day is our assumption, not a measured viewing average. 76.9 × 5 = 384.5 Wh, which this page rounds to 384 Wh.

Panels = 384 Wh ÷ (peak sun hours × 0.75). At the U.S. average of 4.98 hours, 4.98 × 0.75 = 3.735, and 384 ÷ 3.735 = 103 W.

Battery, one day = 384 Wh ÷ (0.9 depth of discharge × 0.9 inverter allowance) = 384 ÷ 0.81 = 474 Wh, reported as 0.47 kWh.

Both factors are the site methodology. They are planning allowances, not a lab measurement of this TV. A soundbar, a streaming box, and a brighter picture mode are outside the 76.9 W.

The panels do not run the TV at night

A television is usually on after sunset. The battery supplies the watt-hours while you watch, and the panels replace that energy on a later day with sun. A panel-only setup with no storage does not cover an evening of TV. 103 W is the recharge size for 384 Wh. The draw while the set is on is 76.9 W. ENERGY STAR does not publish startup watts for this model. A TV is not a compressor load the way a refrigerator is, so the missing surge figure is a smaller unknown than on the fridge pages, and it is still unpublished.

If this set sits in the listed standby for the other 19 hours, 0.30 × 19 = 5.7 Wh, about 1.5% of the 384 Wh on-mode day. Quick-start modes on other sets can idle higher. The 0.30 W number is this model family.

76.9 W is one certified set, not the middle of the list

On November 6, 2026 that ENERGY STAR dataset had 21 certified models with a viewable diagonal from 54 to 56 inches. On-mode power ran from 64.4 W to 113.6 W, and the median was 95.5 W. At the same 5-hour assumption, 95.5 × 5 = 477.5 Wh. That is 477.5 ÷ 3.735 = about 128 W of panels and 477.5 ÷ 0.81 = about 0.59 kWh of battery, using the U.S. sun hours. Larger classes in the same download drew more: a 65-inch class median of 117.7 W across 23 models, and a 75-inch class median of 144.0 W across 7 models. Certified models are not the whole market. If your set is nearer 95 W than 77 W, size from the higher row or, better, from a plug-in meter over a few real evenings.

Other evening loads, and sun that is not 4.98 hours

A TV rarely runs alone at night. A Wi-Fi router and a laptop add watt-hours to the same battery. The one-day essentials list already includes a TV among other loads: battery for 1 day of essentials. State sun changes only the panel side. At 384 Wh, Hawaii's 5.82 peak sun hours are about 88 W of panels, Oregon's 4.06 hours are about 126 W, Nebraska's 4.98 hours stay at 103 W, and New Jersey's 4.66 hours are 110 W. The climate write-ups are on the Hawaii, Oregon, Nebraska, and New Jersey pages. Home-battery kWh, as opposed to this 0.47 kWh television day, is how much battery storage do I need.

Questions

How many solar panels to run a 55-inch TV?

The worked example is an LG 55QNED85 (ENERGY STAR model family 55QNED85TU*), 76.9 W on-mode at a 54.6-inch viewable diagonal. At an assumed 5 hours a day that is 384 Wh (76.9 times 5 is 384.5, rounded). At 4.98 peak sun hours, panel watts are 384 divided by (4.98 times 0.75), about 103 W, and the one-day battery is 384 divided by 0.81, about 0.47 kWh. Other certified 55-inch-class sets draw more.

Where does the daily energy come from?

76.9 W times 5 hours. Five hours is our assumption, not a measured viewing average. ENERGY STAR lists 76.9 W on-mode and 0.30 W standby with the network disconnected for 55QNED85TU*. Standby with the network connected is also 0.30 W on that listing. On-mode power is what has to be supplied while the TV is on.

Will the panels run the TV at night?

Not by themselves. A television is usually an after-sunset load, so the battery supplies the 384 Wh and the panels recharge it later. A panel-only setup with no storage does not cover an evening of TV. The 103 W figure is the recharge size for that daily energy, not the power the TV draws, which is 76.9 W.

What if my 55-inch TV uses more than 76.9 W?

At 5 hours, 95.5 W is 477.5 Wh, about 128 W of panels and a 0.59 kWh battery at the same U.S. sun and loss factors. That set is certified models only, and the list changes.
