How many solar panels to run a CPAP?

Less than most people expect. An AirSense 11 with the humidifier off uses about 56 Wh a night on a 12 V DC converter, which is about 15 W of panels at the U.S. average. With the HumidAir humidifier and heated tube it is about 440 Wh, or about 118 W of panels, and closer to 270 W in a northern December. Run it from DC, not the AC outlet, or the inverter can use more power than the CPAP.

Medical notice. This page is energy-sizing math from published figures, not medical advice. Don't change your prescribed pressure, humidity or tube settings to stretch a battery; ask your clinician or equipment supplier first. Use only the DC cord or converter your CPAP maker specifies. If you use a ventilator or a device you can't safely go without, follow the backup plan from your clinician and supplier. Which machines run from which sources comes from maker documents; product links on this page don't change it.

Short answer

  • Humidifier off: ResMed measures the AirSense 11 at 7 W in operation. Eight hours is 56 Wh. One 50 W panel covers it most of the year, and a 300 Wh power station holds about four nights.
  • Humidifier on: 19 W with the HumidAir 11 (152 Wh a night, about 41 W of panels). The heated ClimateLineAir tube raises it to 55 W (440 Wh, about 118 W of panels at the U.S. average).
  • Winter is the sizing month: Detroit gets about 2.17 sun hours a day in December, less than half the U.S. annual average. A heated-tube setup needs about 270 W of panels there just to break even.
  • DC beats AC by a wide margin: a power station's inverter uses 15–40 W just to stay on. That is 120–320 Wh over a night, two to six times what a humidifier-off CPAP uses.
  • Plan for cloudy days: size the battery for two or three nights without sun. For a humidifier-off AirSense 11 that is under 200 Wh; with a heated tube it is about 1.5 kWh.

Energy by machine and setting

CPAP energy depends far more on humidification than on the machine. ResMed publishes measured operating power for its current machines: the motor and electronics of an AirSense 10 or 11 run at about 7 W, the HumidAir water-tank heater adds 12–15 W, and the heated tube adds another 12–36 W depending on the machine. Higher pressures, big mask leaks and a cold bedroom push the heaters harder, so your own number can be higher or lower.

DC vs the AC outlet: the inverter is the biggest load

A CPAP runs on 24 V DC internally. Plugged into a power station's AC outlet, the station turns battery power into 120 V AC and the CPAP's brick turns it back into 24 V DC. Each conversion loses a little, but the bigger cost is the inverter itself, which draws power for as long as it is on.

AC adds the inverter's own draw for the full 8 hours: 15 W × 8 h = 120 Wh. 15 W is the low end of published figures (BLUETTI builds about 15 W into its AC180 runtime formula; Anker says the SOLIX F3800 Plus uses about 40 W with AC on and nothing plugged in). At 40 W the humidifier-off night on AC is 376 Wh, almost seven times the DC figure.

For solar sizing this changes the panel count, not just the runtime. A humidifier-off night on AC needs about three times the panels of the same night on DC. With the heated tube on, the inverter is a smaller share (about a fifth of the total), so DC matters less, but it still saves about 120 Wh a night. Willrun covers the full comparison, including UPS switchover and which ports trip, in CPAP on DC vs AC.

Cloudy days and winter: size for the worst week

Annual-average sun hours are fine for a cabin light. A CPAP is used every night, so size for the darkest month and for a few days of overcast. In Detroit, PVWatts puts December at about 2.17 sun hours a day; Phoenix in July gets about 6.80. A 100 W panel therefore makes roughly 160 Wh on an average Detroit December day and about 510 Wh on a Phoenix July day, before clouds.

Battery Wh = nightly Wh × 3 ÷ 0.9. Ah = Wh ÷ 12.8 V (nominal LiFePO4; Battle Born rates its BB10012 for 100% usable depth of discharge, datasheet, we keep 10% in reserve). "Break even" panels refill one night per December day at Detroit's 2.17 sun hours. The last column doubles that: each average December day then makes two nights' worth, one for that night and one to put back, so a drained 3-night bank is full again after about 3 days of average December sun while the CPAP keeps running every night.

A practical rule: give yourself three nights of battery and about twice the break-even panel watts for your worst month, which refills the bank in about three average days. For the heated tube in the North, that is a 1.5 kWh battery and 500–550 W of panels, which is a real system. If that is too much, the simplest saving is to switch the heated tube off on battery nights, but only after asking whoever prescribed your settings.

Use the maker's DC converter

  • ResMed AirSense 11: the Air11 DC/DC converter accepts 12 V or 24 V input (10 A / 5 A) and outputs 24 V at 3.75 A. ResMed says it can run the AirSense 11 with the HumidAir and the heated ClimateLineAir tube.
  • ResMed AirSense 10 and AirCurve 10: the Air10 DC/DC converter 90 W is rated 13 A at 12 V, above the 10 A limit of most station car sockets, and ResMed notes the HumidAir uses considerably more power. Plan on humidity off or a higher-current 12 V source.
  • Philips DreamStation: the DreamStation manual says to use only the Philips DC power cord and battery adapter cable.
  • Current at 12 V: 7 W is well under 1 A; the 55 W heated-tube setup is about 5 A after converter losses. Both fit a 10 A car socket.

How to connect the panel

Never plug a solar panel straight into a CPAP or its DC converter. Panel voltage swings with sun and temperature, and output collapses when a cloud passes. Put a battery in between: a power station with a solar input, or a 12 V LiFePO4 battery with a charge controller. The panel charges the battery during the day; the CPAP runs from the battery at night.

For a power station, match the panel's open-circuit voltage and connector to the station's solar input (most portable stations take 12–60 V on XT60 or Anderson). For a 12 V battery, use an MPPT controller rated for the panel current and a fused 12 V socket for the CPAP converter. Our RV solar guide and battery storage guide walk through both.

What we'd buy

  • Camping, humidifier off: a 250–300 Wh LiFePO4 station with a 12 V car socket (EcoFlow RIVER 3 Plus or Anker SOLIX C300) plus a 100 W folding panel and the maker's DC converter. That is about 4 nights per charge, and a 100 W panel refills a night in under an hour of good sun.
  • Home outages with humidification: a 1 kWh station (EcoFlow DELTA 3 Plus, Anker SOLIX C1000 or Jackery Explorer 1000 v2) plus 200 W of panels for most of the year, or 400 W for winter in the North. That is about 2 heated-tube nights or 6 HumidAir-only nights per charge on DC.
  • Van or RV: a 100 Ah 12.8 V LiFePO4 house battery (1,280 Wh) with 200–400 W on the roof and the ResMed converter on a fused 12 V socket. That covers about 2.6 heated-tube nights or about 20 humidifier-off nights with no sun.

Questions

How many solar panels to run a CPAP?

It depends on the humidifier. A ResMed AirSense 11 uses about 56 Wh a night with the humidifier off, 152 Wh with the HumidAir 11 and 440 Wh with the heated tube. At the U.S. average of 4.98 sun hours that is about 15 W, 41 W and 118 W of panels, if you run the CPAP from a 12 V DC converter.

Where do the figures come from?

ResMed's therapy-device power table, measured in a lab: AirSense 11 AutoSet 7 W in operation and 2 W standby, 19 W with HumidAir 11, 55 W with HumidAir 11 and the heated ClimateLineAir 11 tube; AirMini 6.3 W; AirSense 10 with HumidAir 22 W, with heated tube 34 W. The 56.1 W ceiling is the typical consumption in the AirSense 11 user guide's power supply section.

How big a battery do I need for a CPAP?

For one night, about 60 Wh with the humidifier off and about 490 Wh with the heated tube, keeping a 10% reserve. For three cloudy nights, about 190 Wh and 1,470 Wh. On the AC outlet add the inverter's 15–40 W for every hour it is on.

Can I run a CPAP directly from a solar panel?

No. Panel output changes with every cloud and can drop to nothing. Charge a battery or power station from the panel and run the CPAP from the battery with the maker's DC cord or converter.

Is DC or AC better for running a CPAP on solar?

DC, in almost every case. The inverter behind a power station's AC outlet draws 15–40 W just to stay on, which can be more than the CPAP. Use AC only when you need the station's UPS switchover or your machine's humidifier requires the AC supply, as with the Philips DreamStation.

How many solar panels for a CPAP in winter?

Size for your darkest month. In Detroit in December (about 2.17 sun hours a day) a humidifier-off AirSense 11 needs about 35 W of panels to break even and a heated-tube setup about 270 W. Double that if you want to refill the battery after cloudy days.
