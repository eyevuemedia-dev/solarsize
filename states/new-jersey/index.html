How much solar do I need in New Jersey?

Short answer: New Jersey averages 4.66 peak sun hours and 1,305 kWh a year per kW, so a home using 10 kWh a day needs about 2,861 W of panels. A paired PVGIS run puts Newark and Atlantic City within 1% on the year. The average customer buys 662 kWh a month, which is why a full offset of that bill is about 6.1 kW.

Shore versus Newark is the wrong split

The table above stays on the site formula. Panel watts = daily Wh ÷ (4.66 × 0.75). A 10 kWh day is 10,000 ÷ 3.495 = 2,861 W, about 8 panels of 400 W, and 12.35 kWh of battery for one sunless day. People often ask whether the Shore needs a different array than the urban northeast. These two south-facing points say no.

Newark's year is 1,369 kWh per kW. Atlantic City's is 1,364. That is under a 1% gap. Both sit a few percent above the statewide PVWatts figure of 1,305. The tool-and-roof gap is larger than the city gap. Shade, azimuth, and a roof that does not face south will move your result more than choosing the Shore over Newark. Neighbors on the same state table: New York at 4.50 peak sun hours and 1,291 kWh per kW, and Pennsylvania at 4.79 hours and 1,356 kWh per kW. New Jersey sits between them.

Lower purchases, not better sun, shrink the average system

The average New Jersey residential customer bought 662 kWh a month in 2024, bill $128 (EIA Table 5A). That is 7,944 kWh a year, well under the U.S. average of 863 kWh a month and under Nebraska's 956, even though Nebraska's sun hours (4.98) are only a little higher than New Jersey's 4.66. Divided by 1,305 kWh per kW, covering the average New Jersey purchases takes about 6.1 kW, or 16 panels of 400 W, listed at about 336 square feet in the sizing guide.

A house that buys 1,000 kWh a month is not that average. 12,000 ÷ 1,305 = about 9.2 kW, or 23 panels of 400 W, using the same annual formula. Use your own 12 months. EIA purchases miss generation that never flows through the meter.

Winter still matters if you expected the annual array to cover a dark day. A 2,861 W array makes about 7.4 kWh on an average December day at the Newark rate (2.861 × 2.60) and about 7.1 kWh at Atlantic City (2.861 × 2.47). That is a grid-banking problem, not a reason to buy two different system sizes for those cities. Outage storage is a separate design: how much battery storage do I need. The 0.75 derate and the 0.81 battery factor are on the methodology page. Incentive and credit prices move, and they are not part of the wattage.

Limits

  • Two clear-horizon points understate trees and neighboring buildings, which dominate a lot of New Jersey roofs.
  • December averages are not the worst week. Snow on a shallow roof is outside the PVGIS run.
  • 6.1 kW offsets the average purchase total. It is not a design for a house with different kWh.

Questions

How many peak sun hours does New Jersey get?

4.66 kWh per square meter per day, from NREL PVWatts v8, and 1,305 kWh a year per kW. On this site's state table, New York is 4.50 hours and 1,291 kWh per kW, and Pennsylvania is 4.79 hours and 1,356 kWh per kW.

How many solar panels for a house in New Jersey?

The average New Jersey customer bought 662 kWh a month in 2024 (EIA Table 5A), 7,944 kWh a year, bill about $128. Divided by 1,305 kWh per kW, that is about 6.1 kW, or 16 panels of 400 W. A home that buys 1,000 kWh a month instead needs about 9.2 kW (12,000 divided by 1,305), or 23 panels of 400 W.

Does the Jersey Shore need more panels than Newark?

Not on these two points. South-facing latitude-tilt PVGIS runs give Newark (40.74°N, 74.17°W) 1,369 kWh per kW per year and Atlantic City (39.36°N, 74.42°W) 1,364. That is under a 1% gap. Both sit a few percent above the statewide PVWatts figure of 1,305. Shade, azimuth, and tilt will move a roof more than that city pair did.

Will an annual system cover a December day?

A 2,861 W array sized for 10 kWh a day produces about 7.4 kWh on an average December day at the Newark point (2.861 times 2.60) and about 7.1 kWh at Atlantic City. Annual sizing assumes the grid carries the seasonal difference. It is not an off-grid December design.
