Peak sun hours by state, and the panels for 10 kWh a day

Short answer: Arizona averages 6.54 peak sun hours and Alaska 3.17, from NREL PVWatts v8. The panel column is one comparison load, 10 kWh a day: watts = 10,000 ÷ (peak sun hours × 0.75). It is not the system size for a real bill. A house is yearly kWh ÷ the kWh-per-kW column.

A peak sun hour is a day of sun expressed as hours at 1,000 W per square meter. The hours and the kWh-per-kW column come from the same state table derived from PVWatts v8. They are not the same formula. kWh per kW is PVWatts annual output and already includes PVWatts losses. The panel column applies this site's 0.75 derate to peak sun hours, written up on the methodology page. The two usually land close. For a house, divide a year of kWh by the kWh-per-kW figure. That method, with 2024 EIA purchases for every state, is how many solar panels do I need.

When the state row is the wrong design number

The rank order is a fair way to compare states. It is a poor way to size three kinds of projects.

  • Winter, not the year. Oregon's 4.06 hours tracks a Portland year (about 1,129 kWh per kW in PVGIS, next to the state's 1,121). The 3,284 W array in the 10 kWh row makes about 4.8 kWh on an average December day there. Nebraska matches the 4.98-hour U.S. figure used on the appliance pages. The 2,677 W array in its 10 kWh row still makes about 7.2 kWh on an average December day in Omaha.
  • One side of the state. Bend's PVGIS year is about 1,540 kWh per kW, roughly 37% above Oregon's state figure, so using 4.06 hours east of the Cascades oversizes the array. On Hawaii, a Hilo point is about 21% below a Honolulu point, and the 5.82-hour average sits nearer Honolulu. New Jersey is the counterexample: Newark and Atlantic City annual output match within 1%, so a shore-versus-city split is not what the data show.
  • Purchases, not sun. Hawaii's average residential purchases were 495 kWh a month in 2024. Nebraska's were 956. The sunnier row is not automatically the smaller system. EIA counts utility purchases, which understate use where rooftop solar is already common.

Those four pages keep the original wattage table and add the monthly PVGIS runs, the EIA purchase math, and the limits. Off-grid design throws out the annual column and uses the worst month. That method, for cabin regions rather than these state averages, is the off-grid solar cabin guide.

Questions

What is a peak sun hour in this table?

A peak sun hour is a day's solar resource expressed as hours at 1,000 W per square meter. The hours column is a state average derived from NREL PVWatts v8. Arizona is 6.54. Alaska is 3.17. Oregon is 4.06.

How is the panel-watt column calculated?

Panels for 10 kWh a day equal 10,000 divided by (peak sun hours times 0.75). The 0.75 derate is this site's allowance for temperature, wiring, inverter, and soiling. It is not the same formula as the kWh-per-kW column, which is PVWatts annual output and already includes PVWatts losses. The methodology page writes both down.

When is the state average the wrong design number?

When the question is December rather than the year, or when one side of the state is much cloudier than the other. A Portland PVGIS year matches Oregon's state figure and a Bend year does not. Hilo produces less than Honolulu. Newark and Atlantic City, by contrast, match each other within 1%. Use the state row to compare, then run PVWatts at the address.
