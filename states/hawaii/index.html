How much solar do I need in Hawaii?

Short answer: Hawaii averages 5.82 peak sun hours and 1,622 kWh a year per kW, so a home using 10 kWh a day needs about 2,291 W of panels. EIA's 495 kWh a month is utility purchases, not whole-home use. A Hilo point produces about 21% less per year than a Honolulu point, and December is much closer to summer than it is in Oregon.

5.82 hours is not every shore

The sizing table is unchanged. Panel watts = daily Wh ÷ (5.82 × 0.75). A 10 kWh day is 10,000 ÷ 4.365 = 2,291 W, about 6 panels of 400 W, and 12.35 kWh of battery for one sunless day. The battery line does not shrink just because the latitude is low. What does change is how flat the year is, and how far a windward point sits from a leeward one.

Honolulu's year, 1,668 kWh per kW, is a few percent above the state PVWatts figure of 1,622. Hilo's 1,315 is about 21% below Honolulu and about 19% below the state figure. The state average behaves more like leeward Oʻahu than like windward Hawaiʻi Island. December in Honolulu is 3.97 kWh per kW against 5.13 in August, a much smaller swing than Portland's 1.46 in December against 4.57 in July. Hilo's lowest month in this run is November, at 3.14, not a mainland-style December collapse.

The 3.7 kW "average home" is purchases

The average Hawaii residential customer bought 495 kWh a month in 2024, and the average bill was $212 (EIA Table 5A). That is 5,940 kWh a year. Divided by 1,622 kWh per kW, covering those purchases takes about 3.7 kW, or 10 panels of 400 W. $212 ÷ 495 kWh is about 43¢ per purchased kWh. That ratio of the two EIA averages is not a tariff, and it is not a quote.

EIA's own FAQ says household averages count electricity bought from the grid. States with a lot of rooftop solar, Hawaii included, show lower "use" than the home actually consumes, because generation behind the meter never hits the bill. A 3.7 kW system offsets a typical purchase total. It does not describe a house that uses 800 or 1,000 kWh and exports the difference, and it does not describe a house whose bill is already small because panels are on the roof. If you already have solar, the bill is net purchases. Size from a year of gross use if you can get it from the monitoring app, not from the residual utility kWh.

A 2,291 W array sized for a flat 10 kWh a day produces about 9.1 kWh on an average December day at the Honolulu rate (2.291 × 3.97) and about 7.2 kWh at Hilo's November rate (2.291 × 3.14). Annual sizing still assumes you can use or be credited for the surplus months. This page does not price export credits or utility program rules; those change, and they are not an input to the wattage. For the separate question of whether a grid-tied array runs in an outage, see grid-tie versus battery backup. Off-grid worst-month method, which Hawaii's mild swing only partly relaxes, is the cabin guide. Loss factors are on the methodology page, and the 50-state purchase table is how many solar panels do I need.

Limits

  • Two points are not an island survey. Trade-wind slopes, volcanic haze, and roof azimuth are outside this pair.
  • PVGIS has no snow term here; the real local losses are cloud and terrain, which the horizon setting only partly captures.
  • No incentive or export-credit dollars are included. Confirm the current program with the utility before choosing a 100% offset.

Questions

How many peak sun hours does Hawaii get?

5.82 kWh per square meter per day on average, from NREL PVWatts v8. A Honolulu PVGIS run is about 1,668 kWh per kW per year. A Hilo run is about 1,315. The state figure sits nearer Honolulu than Hilo.

How many solar panels for a house in Hawaii?

EIA's 2024 Hawaii average is 495 kWh of residential purchases a month and a $212 bill. Dividing 5,940 kWh a year by 1,622 kWh per kW gives about 3.7 kW, or 10 panels of 400 W. Those 495 kWh are purchases. Homes that already have solar, or that use more than they buy, are not described by that average.

How large is the Honolulu to Hilo gap?

For 1 kW, south-facing, tilt equal to latitude, PVGIS-NSRDB gives Honolulu (21.31°N, 157.86°W) about 1,668 kWh a year and a December day of 3.97 kWh. Hilo (19.73°N, 155.09°W) gives about 1,315 kWh a year and a lowest month of 3.14 kWh in November. Hilo's year is about 21% below Honolulu's in this pair. The seasonal swing in both places is much smaller than in Oregon.

Does a mild winter mean the battery column goes away?

No. The 12.35 kWh battery for a 10 kWh day is storage for hours with no sun, including evening. Hawaii's December is close to its summer, so the seasonal gap is smaller than on the mainland, but a panel-only system still produces nothing at night.
