How much solar do I need in Oregon?

Short answer: Oregon averages 4.06 peak sun hours and 1,121 kWh a year per kW, so a home using 10 kWh a day needs about 3,284 W of panels. That statewide year matches cloudy western Oregon. A Bend point produces about 1,540 kWh per kW, and the same 3,284 W array makes only about 4.8 kWh on an average December day in Portland.

The 4.06-hour average is a west-side number

The table above is unchanged: panel watts = daily Wh ÷ (4.06 × 0.75), and the battery column is daily kWh ÷ (0.9 × 0.9). A 10 kWh day is 10,000 ÷ 3.045 = 3,284 W, about 9 panels of 400 W, and 12.35 kWh of battery for one day with no sun. Yearly energy tells a different story than that single sun-hour figure, because the Cascades split the state.

Western Oregon behaves like the state row and like Washington (3.95 peak sun hours, 1,088 kWh per kW). East of the Cascades, using 4.06 hours oversizes the array.

December is not the annual average

A grid-tied system sized at 3,284 W is an annual plan. Scaled by the Portland December rate, 3.284 kW × 1.46 kWh per kW is about 4.8 kWh on an average December day, not 10. The same array at the Bend rate is 3.284 × 2.45, about 8.0 kWh. July in Portland is 3.284 × 4.57, about 15 kWh. Annual sizing works only if the grid banks the summer surplus, through net metering or whatever export rule your utility actually uses. This page does not price that credit. An off-grid cabin cannot bank it. Size that for the worst month, which the cabin guide already does for Bend (December 2.64 kWh per kW at a 59° tilt) and which the Portland row above shows is a harder problem on the west side.

The two published yearly methods are close and not identical. 10 kWh × 365 = 3,650 kWh a year, and 3,650 ÷ 1,121 = 3.26 kW. The peak-sun-hour formula gives 3.28 kW. The difference is the 0.75 derate versus PVWatts' own loss stack. Keep the table; use PVWatts at the roof for a bid.

What the average Oregon bill is

The average Oregon residential customer bought 882 kWh a month in 2024, and the average bill was $130 (EIA Table 5A). That is 10,584 kWh a year. Divided by 1,121 kWh per kW, covering those purchases takes about 9.4 kW, or 24 panels of 400 W. EIA counts utility purchases, so a home that already has solar can look smaller than the electricity it uses. One month × 12 also misses whether the kWh fall in a dark winter or a bright summer. Add the bill's kWh for all 12 months. The same method for every state is in how many solar panels do I need, and the loss factors are on the methodology page. Battery energy for outages, as opposed to this annual offset, is in how much battery storage do I need.

Limits

  • The state row is not a roof. Azimuth, tilt, shade from firs, and snow (which PVGIS ignores) move December more than the annual total.
  • No incentive, tax credit, or utility-rebate dollars are included. Those programs change and they do not change the wattage math.
  • Have a licensed installer design any grid-tied or permanent system. These are planning estimates.

Questions

How many peak sun hours does Oregon get?

4.06 kWh per square meter per day on average, from NREL PVWatts v8 data in the state table. A south-facing Portland point in PVGIS produces about 1,129 kWh per kW per year, within 1% of the state's 1,121. A Bend point produces about 1,540, so 4.06 hours describes the west side.

How many solar panels for a house in Oregon?

The average Oregon customer bought 882 kWh a month in 2024 (EIA Table 5A), 10,584 kWh a year. Divided by 1,121 kWh per kW, that is about 9.4 kW, or 24 panels of 400 W. A 10 kWh-a-day load, using peak sun hours times 0.75, is 3,284 W. Run the address in PVWatts before you buy, especially east of the Cascades.

How much less sun does Portland get than Bend in December?

At a south-facing tilt equal to latitude, PVGIS-NSRDB puts an average December day at 1.46 kWh per kW in Portland (45.52°N, 122.68°W, 46° tilt) and 2.45 kWh per kW in Bend (44.06°N, 121.31°W, 44° tilt). The Bend December figure matches the off-grid cabin guide. PVGIS does not model snow on the panels.

Will 3,284 W deliver 10 kWh in December?

Not on an average December day at the Portland point. 3.284 kW times 1.46 kWh per kW is about 4.8 kWh. The same array at the Bend point is about 8.0 kWh. The 3,284 W figure is an annual planning size. It assumes the grid absorbs the seasonal difference. An off-grid cabin has to be sized for the worst month instead.
